## 1. Verbal Communication

**Communication** = sending a message from a **sender** to a **receiver** through a **channel**, and getting **feedback**. It's a **two-way process**.

```
Sender → (encodes) → Message → Channel → (decodes) → Receiver
   ↑_____________________ Feedback _____________________|
```
> 📞 **Analogy:** a phone call. If the line is crackly (noise) or the other person speaks a different language (decoding problem), the message doesn't get through, even though you "said" it.

**Factors in the process:** sender, message, channel (face-to-face, email, video…), receiver, feedback, context, and **noise** (anything that distorts the message).

**Verbal communication** = using words (spoken or written). Key ideas:
- **Know your audience:** a talk about cloud computing sounds different for first-year students than for CEOs.
- Be **clear, concise, concrete, correct, courteous, complete** (the "7 Cs" with *considerate*).
- Most valued soft skills: listening, clear speaking, teamwork, adaptability, empathy.

*Example:* "The system is down." ❌ vague → "The student portal has been offline since 9 AM; IT expects it back by noon." ✅

## 2. Non-Verbal Communication

Everything you communicate **without words**. People often trust your body over your words when the two disagree.

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Proxemics** | use of personal space | intimate (< 0.5 m), personal (0.5–1.2 m), social (1.2–3.5 m), public (> 3.5 m) |
| **Orientation** | how you position yourself relative to others | sitting side-by-side = friendly; directly opposite = more confrontational (interviews, interrogations) |
| **Eye contact** | where and how long you look | steady eye contact shows confidence and honesty; too much feels aggressive |
| **Facial expression** | the face shows emotion | smile vs frown |
| **Gesture** | hand and arm movements | thumbs up; pointing can be rude in some cultures |
| **Dress** | clothing and appearance | formal attire for an interview signals seriousness |
| **Posture** | how you sit or stand | upright = confident; slouched = bored |
| **Paralanguage** | *how* you say it: tone, pitch, speed, volume, pauses | "Great." said cheerfully vs sarcastically |
| **Haptics** | touch | handshake, pat on the back |
| **Chronemics** | use of time | being late to a meeting sends a message |
| Colours / flowers | symbolic meaning | red = danger or love; white = purity or mourning (culture-dependent) |

> 🎭 **Analogy:** words are the lyrics, non-verbal cues are the music. The same lyrics can sound happy or sad depending on the tune.

## 3. Barriers to Communication & Group Skills

**Communication barriers** = anything that blocks or distorts the message ("noise").

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical** | loud room, bad Wi-Fi, distance, closed office doors |
| **Language / information** | jargon ("ping the API endpoint"), different languages, ambiguous words |
| **Workplace** | hierarchy, information overload (200 emails a day) |
| **Cultural** | different norms for eye contact, gestures, directness |
| **Psychological / mindset** | stress, emotions, prejudice, **stereotyping**, distrust, deceit, **dialogue cut-offs** (interrupting, dismissing) |

> 🧱 **Tip:** reduce barriers by choosing the right channel, avoiding jargon, checking understanding ("so what you're saying is…"), and staying open-minded.

### Group skills
Working in teams needs skills for both **leading** and **participating**:
- **Task skills:** set goals, give and seek information, summarise, keep time.
- **Maintenance skills:** encourage others, mediate conflict, make sure quiet members get heard.
- **Avoid:** dominating, blocking ideas, side-chatting, free-riding.

> ⚽ **Analogy:** a football team. Talented individuals still lose if nobody passes the ball.

## 4. Listening Skills

**Hearing** is passive (sound enters your ears); **listening** is active (you focus, understand, and respond).

**Three listening modes:**
1. **Attentive:** genuinely interested and focused.
2. **Responsive:** showing you're listening (nodding, "mm-hmm", questions).
3. **Active:** paraphrasing, clarifying, summarising to confirm understanding.

**Common problems:** *crossed wires* (each person means something different) and *wrong wavelength* (talking at different levels, e.g. facts vs feelings).

**Tips:** stop talking, remove distractions, don't interrupt, ask open questions, summarise ("So, the main issue is…"), listen for **meaning**, not just words. Remember names by repeating them ("Nice to meet you, Hafiz").

> 🎧 **Analogy:** active listening is like being a good mirror. You reflect back what you heard so the speaker can confirm it.

## 5. Presentation Skills

### Preparation
- **Know your audience and purpose:** inform, persuade, or entertain?
- **Research** the topic thoroughly; prepare **index cards** with key points (not a full script).
- Timing: ~**100 words per minute**, so a 20-minute talk ≈ 2,000 words. About **one slide per minute**.
- **Rehearse** out loud, ideally in front of someone.

### Structure ("tell them what you'll tell them, tell them, tell them what you told them")
1. **Introduction:** hook (question, statistic, story), purpose, roadmap.
2. **Body:** 3–5 main points, each with evidence or examples.
3. **Conclusion:** summarise, call to action, thank the audience, Q&A.

**Signposting language:** "First…", "Moving on to…", "To sum up…". It helps the audience follow along.

### Delivery
- **Visual aids:** simple slides, large fonts, few words per slide, charts over tables. Slides support you; they're not your script.
- **Non-verbal:** eye contact around the room, open posture, natural gestures, vary your voice, pause for emphasis.
- **Handling questions:** listen fully, repeat the question, answer briefly, and admit it if you don't know ("Good question, I'll find out and get back to you").
- **Leave-behinds:** handouts for after the talk.

> 🎤 **Analogy:** a presentation is a guided tour. You're the tour guide; the slides are the signposts, not the destination.

## 6. Persuasive Language & Propaganda

**Persuasive language** tries to change what people think or do.

| Technique | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Vagueness** | unclear on purpose | "Helps reduce wrinkles" (by how much?) |
| **Ambiguity** | more than one meaning | "Nothing works better than our pill" |
| **Emotive language** | words chosen for their feeling | "brutal attack" vs "incident" |
| **Advertising language** | "new", "free", "proven", "limited time" | "Clinically proven!" |
| **Euphemism** | soften something harsh | "let go" instead of "fired" |
| **Dysphemism** | make something sound worse | "cancer stick" for a cigarette |
| **Stereotyping** | oversimplified labels for groups | "All gamers are lazy" |

**Propaganda techniques:** bandwagon ("everyone's buying it"), testimonial (celebrity endorsement), glittering generalities ("freedom", "best"), plain folks ("I'm just like you"), name-calling, card stacking (only showing the good side), transfer (linking a product to positive symbols like the flag).

> 🛒 Recognising these helps you resist manipulation in ads, politics and social media.

## 7. Critical Thinking

**Critical thinking** = thinking clearly and rationally about what to believe or do, by evaluating evidence and reasoning instead of accepting things at face value.

**A critical thinker:** asks questions, looks for evidence, considers other viewpoints, recognises their own biases, is open-minded yet sceptical, and reaches well-reasoned conclusions.

**Universal intellectual standards:** clarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, fairness.

### Barriers to critical thinking
| Barrier | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Egocentrism** | seeing everything from your own point of view | "My way is obviously right" |
| **Sociocentrism** | assuming your group is superior | "Our university/country does it best" |
| **Unwarranted assumptions** | believing things without good reason | "He's quiet, so he must be shy" |
| **Wishful thinking** | believing something because you want it to be true | "I'll pass without studying" |
| Relativism, stereotyping, peer pressure, fear of change | | |

> 🔍 **Analogy:** a critical thinker is like a detective. They don't arrest the first suspect; they examine the clues.

## 8. Arguments

An **argument** is a set of statements where some (**premises**) give reasons to support another (**conclusion**).

```
Premise 1: All UM CS students take FOP.
Premise 2: Siti is a UM CS student.
Conclusion: Siti takes FOP.
```
**Indicator words:** conclusion → "therefore, so, thus, hence"; premise → "because, since, given that".

| Type | Description | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | if the premises are true, the conclusion *must* be true | **valid / invalid**; *sound* = valid + true premises |
| **Inductive** | premises make the conclusion *probable* | **strong / weak**; *cogent* = strong + true premises |

*Inductive example:* "The sun has risen every day in recorded history, so it will rise tomorrow."

(See also [Propositional Logic](CM-computing-mathematics.md#2-propositional-logic), which formalises valid arguments.)

## 9. Logical Fallacies

A **fallacy** is an error in reasoning that makes an argument look convincing when it isn't.

### A. Fallacies that appeal to emotion
| Fallacy | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Appeal to fear / force** | scare people into agreeing | "Vote for me or crime will explode." |
| **Appeal to vanity / flattery** | flatter people into agreeing | "Smart people like you obviously use our product." |
| **Bandwagon** (numbers / peer pressure) | it's true or good because many people do it | "Millions use it, so it must be good." |
| **Appeal to pity** | use sympathy instead of reasons | "Please don't fail me, I've had a hard semester." |

### B. Fallacies that distract
| Fallacy | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | attack the person, not the argument | "Why listen to her opinion on climate? She failed science." |
| **Red herring** | change the subject | "Why worry about pollution when there's unemployment?" |
| **Straw man** | twist the opponent's argument into a weaker one | "You want less homework? So you think students shouldn't learn?" |

### C. Other common fallacies
| Fallacy | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Begging the question** (circular) | the conclusion is used as a premise | "He's trustworthy because he says he's honest." |
| **Slippery slope** | one step will inevitably lead to disaster | "If we allow phones in class, soon nobody will learn anything." |
| **False dilemma** (black/white) | only two options presented when more exist | "You're either with us or against us." |
| **Hasty generalisation** | conclusion from too few examples | "Two Ubers were late, so all Uber drivers are unreliable." |
| **Post hoc** (false cause) | A happened before B, so A caused B | "I wore my lucky socks and passed, so the socks work." |
| **Appeal to authority** | an authority outside their expertise | "A famous actor says this diet works." |

> 🧠 **Tip:** when you hear an argument, ask: *Is it attacking the idea or the person? Are there really only two options? Is the evidence enough?*

## 10. Problem Solving & Decision Making

### Seven key terms
| Term | Meaning | Example (purpose: graduate with first class) |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | what you want to achieve | CGPA ≥ 3.67 |
| **Situation** | current circumstances (neutral) | CGPA is 3.3, two semesters left |
| **Problem** | the part of the situation blocking the purpose | GPA too low |
| **Cause** | specific facts creating the problem | weak in maths courses, poor time management |
| **Solvable cause** | causes you can actually act on | time management (you can't change past grades) |
| **Issue** | the problem rephrased **positively** | "improve study habits" |
| **Solution** | specific actions | study 2 h daily, join a maths study group |

### Types of problems
- **Well-defined:** clear start, rules, and goal (`3x = 2, solve for x`).
- **Ill-defined:** uncertain information or goal (writing a term paper, building a career).
- Classes: **inducing structure** (find the pattern), **transformation** (water jug puzzle), **arrangement** (anagrams: "lespo" → "slope").

### Types of solutions
- **Algorithm:** a step-by-step procedure that guarantees a solution (only for well-defined problems). *Mean = sum ÷ n.*
- **Heuristic:** a rule of thumb; fast but not guaranteed. *"In blackjack, stay at 15 or more."*

### Problem-solving methods
1. **Rational thinking:** set the ideal → identify the current situation → compare to find the problem → break it into causes → generate solutions → evaluate and choose → implement.
2. **System thinking:** view the problem as a system: `Input → Function → Output`. If the output doesn't meet the purpose, look for **inside causes** (within the system) and **outside causes** (environment).

### Decision-making process
1. Define the decision and its objectives.
2. Generate alternatives, e.g. with **PMI** (de Bono): list **P**lus, **M**inus, and **I**nteresting points.
3. Evaluate alternatives, e.g. with **ALU**: **A**dvantages, **L**imitations, **U**nique connections.
4. Select the best option.
5. Implement (resources, action steps, people, timeline) and review.

*PMI example: "Should I take a part-time job?"*
- **P:** extra income, work experience
- **M:** less study time, fatigue
- **I:** could network for future internships

> 🧭 **Analogy:** decision-making is planning a road trip. Know your destination, compare routes, pick one, then check the map as you go.
